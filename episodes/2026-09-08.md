[serious] Adobe has now released patches for the Commerce and Magento zero-day we were waiting on yesterday. That's the important change: there's a fix to deploy, and the attacks include a Rust backdoor and a P-H-P web shell. CVE-2026-75650 scores ten. It's a template-engine flaw reachable over the network, with no login and no click, giving an attacker code execution as the application's current user. [calm] The affected Commerce branches run from 2 point 4 point 4 through 2 point 4 point 9, including their August twenty twenty-six updates; Magento Open Source branches 2 point 4 point 6 through 2 point 4 point 9 are listed too, alongside Commerce B-to-B releases. Match your branch against Adobe's advisory and apply Monday's security patch. Then investigate the store for both payload types. Closing the entry point doesn't evict what's already inside.

[short pause] And there's a useful follow-up for N-central administrators: N-able is advising you to look for newly created user accounts you don't recognise. [confident] Add that to the incident checks around the emergency update. An unexplained account on a remote-management server deserves investigation, not a quiet deletion that takes the evidence with it.

[serious] Elsewhere, a supposed Google verification file has turned up executing from WebDAV at a Ukrainian government organisation. That observation started Cisco Talos's investigation into a ClearFake infection chain delivering Amatera stealer, ZigCryptoStealer and NetSupport Manager. The file was a D-L-L named verification dot google. [short pause] Quite a lot of reassurance packed into a filename. If you're investigating suspicious verification prompts, check for D-L-L execution from WebDAV locations and for those follow-on payloads. NetSupport Manager is legitimate remote-control software; in that company, its presence needs explaining.

[curious] Same lure family, different execution space: another Talos investigation follows ClickFix into the browser itself. This cryptocurrency-theft campaign retrieves obfuscated JavaScript from a publicly published Google Sheets document through the Google Visualisation A-P-I, then injects it into the victim's browser session. [serious] Google is hosting the retrieval path, not vouching for the code. For browser-based crypto theft investigations, inspect the script retrieval and injection, rather than clearing the traffic just because its destination belongs to Google. A reputable domain can still be carrying somebody else's instructions.

[short pause] And attackers are still working on how to get people to the lure in the first place. BengalSEO poisons Bing search results to deliver MayaBot and tech-support scams. The D-F-I-R Report discovered the campaign in March. [calm] If you're tracing one of those infections or a support-scam incident, preserve the search-to-landing-page chain. The page the user trusted may have been the search results, not the site that eventually delivered the trouble.

[curious] One more browser wrinkle: researchers have disclosed PEEP, a Chromium-based post-exploitation toolkit disguised as a bookmarks extension. It turns Chrome and Edge into backdoors for host command execution. [serious] Post-exploitation matters here: this isn't evidence that simply opening Chrome gets you compromised. But during an incident, extension inventory belongs alongside your other persistence checks. A bookmarks label is a description, not a security boundary.

[long pause] Now to the new flaws, and SAP has two that warrant a proper exposure check. The first sits in its Extended Passport Protocol processing library, which handles SAP tracing information passed between components. CVE-2026-44756 scores ten and accepts a crafted network request with a malformed header, without a login or user interaction. [serious] It's a memory-safety failure with potentially high impact, but the specific outcome described is undefined behaviour and abnormal termination—not a demonstrated remote shell. Don't turn the score into a claim the evidence doesn't make.

[calm] The listed Extended Passport processing versions are 7 point 22 EXT, 7 point 53, 8 point 04, and 9 point 18 through 9 point 20. Check SAP's advisory against those components and restrict unnecessary network reachability while establishing remediation. [short pause] The second flaw attacks trust rather than memory: NetWeaver Message Server, which coordinates application-server communication, doesn't sufficiently authenticate internal components when they register. An unauthenticated attacker can register an unauthorised component and potentially act inside the application environment. CVE-2026-58240 scores nine point eight and affects versions 9 point 18, 9 point 19 and 9 point 20. No fixed releases have been named for either SAP issue here. For Message Server, pay particular attention to which networks can reach the registration service.

[serious] From business applications to electrical protection equipment: Siemens Reyrolle 7SR5 devices before version 2 point 70 expose information through their web interface that lets an attacker calculate current and past session identifiers. These are protection relays used in power systems, so unauthorised device access isn't merely an administrative inconvenience. CVE-2026-62645 scores nine point eight and is reachable over the network without credentials or a click. [calm] Upgrade to 2 point 70 or later through your operational change process, and limit web-interface access while arranging it. Predictable session identifiers rather defeat the point of having a login.

[short pause] There's also a distinct new D-Link DIR-822A flaw, beyond yesterday's warning: firmware A underscore 101 has an out-of-bounds write in its L-two-T-P control-message parser. It requires a low-privilege login over the network, and a public exploit is available. [calm] D-Link haven't identified safe firmware here; assess exposure of that service and check their guidance for remediation.

[long pause] Finally, an exposed Vietnam-linked Advance Passenger Information System database held two hundred and twenty million passenger and crew records spanning twenty seventeen to twenty twenty-six. Names, passport numbers, dates of birth, nationalities and flight details were included. [serious] That's records, not necessarily two hundred and twenty million distinct people. These systems collect travel and identity information together; an exposed copy can therefore reveal far more than a contact list. If you operate passenger-data integrations, check the access controls on the databases receiving those feeds, not just the application in front of them.

[short pause] And a different kind of data-handling consequence in the UK: Grindr has opted to pay twenty-six million pounds to settle claims that it shared personal information, including H-I-V status, with third parties. [serious] Those are allegations being settled, not a newly disclosed intrusion. For teams integrating analytics or advertising services, the relevant question is exactly which sensitive fields leave the application—and whether those transfers belong there at all.

[confident] I'd give the Adobe patch the first change slot, with compromise checks attached, and get SAP service exposure in front of the teams who own it.
