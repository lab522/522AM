# Attackers take the servers that control the firewalls

*GreyNoise has tracked compromises of Check Point management servers at twenty-two organisations, with credentials and internal certificate authority files stolen. There's another vCenter authentication bypass to patch, alongside fresh exploitation alerts and exposed Radware log-export credentials.*

Good morning, it's 5:22 AM. Wednesday, the twenty-fifth of November, twenty twenty-six.

The attackers didn't break the firewall; they took the server that tells it what to allow. GreyNoise says its GT13 actor compromised twenty-four Check Point Security Management servers at twenty-two organisations across seventeen countries. These hold the gateway policy database and the internal certificate authority the estate trusts. The attackers stole those, along with credentials. In one environment, they unloaded the security policy, effectively disabling firewall protection. That's the bit I'd lose sleep over.

This isn't the earlier DynamicCLI incident. GreyNoise couldn't establish the initial entry point, but saw the attackers subsequently use CVE-2026-16232, July's SmartConsole authentication bypass, to obtain an administrator login token. Its confirmed visibility runs from the seventeenth of October to the second of November; it believes persistent access means the campaign continues. If you run Check Point management servers, verify July's protections and hunt with GreyNoise's indicators. Review SmartConsole administrator logins and certificate operations back to mid-October. For an affected server, treat its contents as stolen: rotate the credentials it held and reissue internal CA material. Then check the installed firewall policy against what you intended, not merely whether the gateway looks healthy.

And the other console controlling rather a lot underneath it needs attention again. Broadcom's twenty-three-flaw batch includes CVE-2026-123903, another nine point eight authentication bypass in vCenter's directory service. Broadcom says it's distinct from last week's bug: network access to the service can become unauthorised system access, without credentials. No exploitation is known for this one. ESX also gets three critical fixes, including flaws that could allow host code execution. The new fixed builds are 9 point 1 point 2 and 9 point 0 point 2 point 0300; Cloud Foundation's is 5 point 2 point 5. Already installed vCenter 8 point 0 U3m last week? You're covered for vCenter — a small mercy. On 7 point 0, contact Broadcom Support. Other affected VMware products still need their updates, and last week's SDDC Manager fixes remain a separate job.

Away from management consoles, there's confirmed exploitation at the package registry hex dot pm: on Monday, a crafted Erlang request body exploited CVE-2026-129514 to run operating-system commands without authentication. If you self-host hexpm, update past commit 379b0a29 and treat instances left unpatched before Monday as potentially compromised. And Friday's Drupal installer story has now reached KEV on evidence of active exploitation, with a Friday federal deadline shared by Idunno AntiCsrf and Optimizely Web Experimentation. Apply Drupal's update today if outstanding, and block install dot php at the web server even afterwards.

From compromised applications to the keys they keep: Radware says an internet-exposed, unauthenticated internal Cloud WAF management API leaked customer contact details and some log-export credentials; customers have reported unfamiliar attempts against their AWS buckets. Rotate whichever export credentials you've configured, whether for S3 or Azure Blob Storage, or an SFTP destination, and review storage access logs back to at least the nineteenth of November. Meanwhile, Inception's extortion campaign is obtaining SSO access by phone: Okta and ReversingLabs link it to twenty-one phishing domains capturing passwords, then codes or push approvals. Use phishing-resistant authentication and verify callers before help-desk resets; review identity-provider activity alongside DNS and proxy logs back to the twenty-first of October.

That pressure on exposed services is also showing up in the NCSC's incident reporting: it says AI-assisted attacks have risen markedly since September, causing some service downtime or system compromise. Put exposed services behind identity-aware access with phishing-resistant authentication where possible, prioritise their patches, and investigate broad request bursts with unusually high error rates. Which makes Horizon3's change-freeze example rather pointed: fifty days after we covered Zammad's exploited session flaw, some estates still haven't patched it. I'd make that exception request today, then verify the vendor update actually stops unauthenticated session disclosure.

But don't let old feed traffic compete with that work: Mozilla's replay now reaches MFSA 2005-01 through MFSA 2006-47. Extend archive-date suppression to that earlier boundary; even the exploit-code references belong to those old advisories. By contrast, a genuinely recent browser threat put a card skimmer inside a shared Naviga payment widget on about eighty US newspaper sites, live from the thirteenth to the twenty-second of November and loading roughly fifteen thousand times daily. For payment-page owners, inventory and monitor third-party scripts, restricting outbound connections including WebRTC; if you entered card details on an affected subscription page, watch your statement.

I'd give the management-server investigation an owner before the morning patch meeting. And I'd keep the change-freeze exceptions moving — an exploited flaw doesn't observe the shopping calendar. Hope Wednesday gives you a clear run at it.
