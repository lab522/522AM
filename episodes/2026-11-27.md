# The patch wasn't the finish line

*Drupal's installer fix needs replacing, and MiCollab's exploitation timeline means patched servers still need hunting. Browser malware hijacks management controls to read mail, while repository poisoning and Hex.pm's post-mortem put developer access under scrutiny.*

Good morning, it's 5:22 AM. Friday, the twenty-seventh of November, twenty twenty-six.

That Drupal installer fix we covered on Wednesday hasn't finished the job. A second flaw, CVE-2026-136716, leaves sites in unusual or incomplete installation states open to takeover, including production sites, and attackers are already exploiting it. The uncomfortable bit: the user interface won't show you whether your site is in that state. A compromised site may no longer look vulnerable either. I'd reopen the change ticket, even if last week's deployment was spotless.

The replacement fixes are 11 point 4 point 14 and 11 point 3 point 23; for the supported Drupal ten branch, it's 10 point 6 point 23. Then run update.php: affected production sites need that update hook to finish the repair. Older unsupported branches get no fix. Drupal says sites whose installer was already secured aren't impacted, so keep install.php locked down on existing sites and new ones. Now audit user one: check for unexpected changes by or to that account, confirm its email belongs to you, and consider resetting its password. Review access logs too. StepSecurity counts about fifty-five thousand eight hundred internet-facing Drupal sites, with thirty-one thousand five hundred on branches vulnerable to the original flaw. Roughly fifteen thousand are still on end-of-life Drupal eight and nine.

And there's another reason a completed patch ticket isn't an incident all-clear. Yesterday's MiCollab flaws now have a zero-day timeline: Accenture found a web shell deployed the day before Mitel announced patches on November sixteenth. One victim saw command execution after twelve minutes and root within half an hour; roughly twenty hours in, attackers had reached backup and database servers, with identity infrastructure also reached. Another victim's server had contacted the same infrastructure since November ninth. That's where I'd start the hunt, looking for web shells and unexpected privileged activity rather than stopping at the installed version. Researchers suspect extortion. Attacker infrastructure also contacted two commercial AI services, but sampled telemetry can't establish what they did with them. And Mitel's advisory, updated on November twenty-fourth, still didn't mention exploitation. That's a consequential omission.

The route into a mailbox can be much less technical-looking: a free animated wallpaper that actually works. Bitdefender's SYS010 campaign uses Meta adverts impersonating wallpaper apps, then rewrites management policies in Chrome, Edge and Firefox to force-install extensions users can't remove. Chrome gets enrolled in an attacker-controlled admin console; Edge can involve attacker-run device management. Need administrator approval? The malware repeats the UAC prompt every three seconds, up to ninety-nine times. Persistent little nuisance. The Chrome extension reads Gmail and Outlook messages and can permanently delete selected mail, catching login codes while hiding alerts. An unexplained managed-browser badge deserves an incident ticket. Check policy settings and management enrolment, hunt for Docs Helper or Document Hub, and invalidate affected browser sessions. Review Meta business access too, especially unfamiliar adverts or spending.

Separate campaign, similar mailbox problem: Remus's advertised Gmail zero-day turned out to be a malicious Chrome extension, according to independent researchers, so look for fake Google Sheets carrying the Google Docs Offline description and requesting access to all websites. Remove it and rebuild the profile; password changes and session revocation don't remove an extension that resumes when you sign back in. Meanwhile, Push Security's GoodGuys phishing offers up to six hundred dollars for forty-five-minute expert interviews, then steals work Google or Okta logins and relays MFA challenges live. Hunt for sign-ins following those invitations; anyone who entered credentials and approved a prompt needs a password reset and active sessions revoked.

From borrowed corporate branding to borrowed developer credibility: the North Korea-linked PolinRider campaign has planted Python loaders in at least seven thousand six hundred and nine GitHub repositories, with force-pushed changes preserving original commit authorship. Hunt for unexpected conftest.py files or small utils packages, including payloads subsequently pushed away, and rotate affected GitHub tokens from a clean device. On the package-service breach we covered Wednesday, Hex.pm's post-mortem says six backend servers were taken over and a fix landed within twenty minutes; its storage review found no unauthorised package modifications, but absent database-read logs leave user-data theft unresolved. Backend credentials have been rotated; Hex users should change their password and reset two-factor authentication, then update clients for the new backend and signing keys.

I'd give Drupal's replacement fix a fresh maintenance window, not leave it behind a closed ticket. For MiCollab, I'd put someone on the pre-patch history while that window's being arranged. Hope Friday gives you room to finish properly.
