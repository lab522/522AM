# The backend did the stealing

*1Password says an attacker used an employee’s GitHub account to make its backend steal customer records, while customer passwords remain safe. Ten newly exploited vulnerabilities bring urgent work for infrastructure teams, alongside firewall attacks and refrigeration sabotage with real-world consequences.*

Good morning, it's 5:22 AM. Thursday, the twenty-sixth of November, twenty twenty-six.

1Password says customer passwords are safe after an attacker stole customer information through an employee’s corporate GitHub account. The nasty part is what happened after that account was breached: malicious code reached backend services, and those services downloaded the customer records. I'd want the deployment trail before anything else. The account was the entrance; the backend’s access made it a data breach. That distinction matters when you're deciding what a compromised developer account can actually reach.

And another everyday application has lost customer data: Todoist confirms that six per cent of customers were affected after an intruder reached production. Password hashes were taken alongside email addresses; account creation dates were included too. FIFA also acknowledges access to a limited set of personal and business information and says it's working with law enforcement. Separately, two exposed databases held more than three point five billion records — records, not necessarily that many people.

For FinCEN, the US Treasury’s financial-crimes bureau, containment means some systems are offline after unauthorised access to a limited subset. That's disrupting regulatory filings, with deadlines extended for affected filers. If your organisation files through FinCEN, check its notices rather than assuming the usual timetable still holds. Allstate is also dealing with a multi-day technology outage ahead of Thanksgiving; there's no established cyberattack cause in today's account.

The consequences are much harder at the University Hospital of Guadeloupe. A suspected ransomware attack has postponed surgery and suspended some cancer treatments, with staff reverting to paper; France’s national cybersecurity agency is investigating. And in France’s supermarkets, an Iranian hacking group reportedly turned up refrigeration temperatures at hundreds of sites in October, destroying millions of euros of food. That's a control-system compromise you can smell. I'd want refrigeration access treated as operational access, not a forgotten facilities login.

That concern extends beyond the cold aisle. Research covering two hundred and thirty-six technology suppliers finds deeper security problems across US water systems, while Latin American coverage describes attacks affecting ports and pipelines, with manufacturing also caught up. An infrastructure-security alliance is urging executives to identify priority assets and test response and recovery. Eclypsium’s November InfraTrust findings explain one awkward dependency: most infrastructure advisories addressing exploited flaws over the past month concern bundled third-party software, rather than vendors’ own code.

Software dependencies are also the bait in a recruitment campaign targeting Windows .NET developers. Researchers found thirteen malicious NuGet packages used in fake take-home assessments to deliver malware and gain access to developers’ machines. An interview exercise doesn't get a free pass onto a workstation holding production credentials. Run unfamiliar assessments in an isolated environment without those secrets. Separately, Microsoft has remediated an Azure flaw following attacks against Norwegian energy companies, but still recommends rotating credentials and secrets. The service fix isn't the end of that job.

Which makes GitGuardian’s finding rather uncomfortable: more than sixty-four per cent of valid secrets discovered in twenty twenty-two still worked in January twenty twenty-six. Fear of breaking dependencies keeps revocation stalled. I'd put the dependency owner on the rotation ticket, rather than let an exposed secret become a permanent exception.

On the people selling recovery, US prosecutors accuse a Florida ransomware-recovery executive of secretly paying attackers, charging victims more and pocketing the difference while misrepresenting the service. Those are allegations, not a conviction. Ask recovery providers to put their methods and payment authorisations in writing. Elsewhere, the US anti-scam surge has targeted more than eight hundred defendants, with the FBI targeting over twenty-nine scam compounds.

And there's movement in the identity of yesterday’s extortion crew, rather than a new set of defensive instructions: an account associated with ShinyHunters claims the group is back, Okta sees possible links to Inception, and Inception denies being ShinyHunters. Similar behaviour isn't proof of shared membership. At the more improbable end of recruitment, Smashing Security covers a Russian accused of spying on Palantir after being recruited through Telegram for fifteen thousand dollars. The alleged assignment involved Starbucks; his explanation when caught involved asking a woman out.

The more consequential recruitment story is machines joining offensive teams. Microsoft says China’s Salt Typhoon is using ARTEX, a publicly available AI-powered offensive framework, with adoption also spreading among North Korean groups. Its technical coverage includes network and host hunting guidance. Wiz adds a useful wrinkle: intent identifiers from ARTEX and Cairn can leak into victim logs. Those counters may help sequence activity, but they have limitations; don't turn one identifier into an attribution verdict.

Swiss and Italian agencies now warn of autonomous AI-driven attacks against critical infrastructure. Malwarebytes also describes agents finding an unpatched flaw after being told to break in, while Schneier points to Daniel Miessler’s tracking of AI-assisted incidents and offensive tooling. Unit 42’s discussion centres on attack speed and defensive baselines, including edge AI. The practical counterpoint from Dark Reading is that privileged bots need access limits just as people do, rather than defenders relying entirely on spotting which is which.

On the defensive side, Horizon3 discusses OpenAI and Cisco’s Shirudo work: agents generating and validating signed eBPF runtime controls that can be applied without taking a host down. Clever direction; I'd still want to know how a bad control gets rolled back. Wiz’s new application detection and response offering ties runtime alerts to code functions and proposes automated investigation down to the file and line. A separate pentesting piece argues for using AI on documentation and reporting, where saving time doesn't require handing over the whole engagement.

The wider AI debate includes insurers warning of a divide between organisations with AI defences and those without, and Risky Business arguing that America needs homegrown open-weight models. Australia, meanwhile, has passed its Digital Duty of Care Act, requiring online providers to establish protections against foreseeable serious harms. That's a legal obligation, not another model launch.

There's lighter reading too: Wiz has a seasonal AI-agent piece, and Talos has its Thanksgiving column. Rapid7’s December tenth webinar will revisit its security predictions; SecurityWeek interviews Western Carolina University’s security and privacy chief Matt Martin about building a programme without an enterprise budget. Gartner announcements name Rapid7 a leader for integrated SOC systems and Microsoft a leader for email security. Procurement reading, rather than a reason to interrupt the patch queue.

That queue now has ten more confirmed exploited flaws in CISA’s catalogue. Begin with the systems controlling physical operations: Danfoss AK-SM 800A controllers manage commercial refrigeration and building services. Through firmware 3 point 3, one authenticated flaw exposes underlying server files through the XML interface, potentially leading to full compromise; another permits remote command execution. Both have a Saturday, November twenty-eighth federal deadline. An authentication flaw affecting versions before 4 point 2 has a December ninth deadline. Get the vendor’s applicable remediation moving, and restrict management access now. CISA also has a critical, unpatched warning for this controller family, so don't treat reaching 4 point 2 as a blanket all-clear. There's no established link here between these specific flaws and the French supermarket attack.

For network control, Juniper’s Session Smart Conductor has an especially direct route to root: its AutomatedProvisioner processes a specially crafted Salt minion identifier as operating-system commands. CVE-2026-103406 is nine point eight and now confirmed exploited; CISA’s entry covers Session Smart Router too. Fixed Conductor builds are 7 point 0 point 5-14-lts or, on the 7 point 1 branch, 7 point 1 point 7-15-sts. The 7 point 2 branch needs 7 point 2 point 3-8r2. Saturday is the federal deadline here as well. An attacker ending up as root on the network’s provisioning machinery deserves an emergency change.

And attackers are already rooting Palo Alto Networks firewalls by chaining PHP2Shell with Fragnesia, a kernel flaw. The targets have management interfaces exposed to the internet. Palo Alto has issued a fix for Fragnesia; apply the relevant PAN-OS update and remove public management access. No fixed build numbers accompany today's account. GeoServer, the server publishing geospatial data, also has two newly patched high-severity flaws under active exploitation according to watchTowr. They can combine with other bugs for unauthenticated code execution. Get the current GeoServer security fixes rather than waiting for a scan to tell you someone tried them.

The conferencing server needs similar urgency. Five MiCollab flaws are now in KEV, all affecting its Audio, Web and Video Conferencing component through 10 point 3 point 0 point 18. An unauthenticated attacker can upload arbitrary files; a separate XML injection flaw can turn pre-staged malicious content into system commands. There's also blind command injection without authentication, and a fail-open authentication bypass permitting unauthorised application actions. Those four have Saturday deadlines. The fifth exposes sensitive information when an attacker knows a particular generated identifier or has pre-staged content; it's confined to the affected functionality, not unrestricted file access, and its deadline is December ninth. Obtain Mitel’s remediation for all five, and restrict access to AWV while that's being arranged.

Then there's a beautifully unpleasant authentication mistake in Xen Orchestra, which manages and backs up XCP-ng or XenServer infrastructure. Cookie parsing accepts an object where the token provider expects a string. An empty object becomes an unfiltered token lookup, returning the first token; if it's unexpired, the attacker becomes its owner. An authenticated endpoint then exposes reusable login token IDs. That's CVE-2026-133469, nine point eight, confirmed exploited and due for federal remediation by Saturday. Update Xen Orchestra to 6 point 9 point 2 or 6 point 10 point 1 for the respective branches. The server package fixes are 5 point 211 point 2 and 5 point 213 point 1. I'd put that beside the other infrastructure-control emergencies, not behind routine application updates.

While you're working through virtualisation, Horizon3 has published analysis of the earlier vCenter directory-service bypass, and Rapid7 has covered the distinct November twenty-fourth advisory. Those are additional technical write-ups, not another new patch batch. A genuinely new compatibility concern is Microsoft’s November NTLM signing changes: some third-party applications and devices can now fail authentication. If logins broke after updating, check that interaction before undoing security updates wholesale.

The newly disclosed router flaws have public exploits, but today's information doesn't establish active attacks. Tenda RX3 firmware 16 point 03 point 13 point 11_multi has command injection in remote web management without authentication, with further injection flaws in MAC settings and IPv6 configuration requiring high privileges. The same multi build has authentication defects in the HTTP security handler and the Wi-Fi setup endpoint. The non-multi 16 point 03 point 13 point 11 build also has a remotely reachable hostname stack overflow during character conversion in auto-discovery. The highest ratings reach ten. No fixed release is identified; restrict access to the affected services and seek Tenda’s remediation rather than assuming a password covers these.

The same public-exploit concern applies to D-Link R32 firmware 1 point 10B02: the HTTP service’s flupl self endpoint lets an unauthenticated remote attacker inject operating-system commands through its file-path parameter. TOTOLINK N350RT has a remote authorisation bypass through the language-setting parameter, affecting 9 point 3 point 5u point 6468_B20250827 and 9 point 3 point 5u point 6466_B20250825. Neither has a fixed build identified here. Keep their administration interfaces away from untrusted networks and ask the vendors for a safe release.

And for web-estate owners, Wordfence’s weekly tally adds five hundred and twenty-seven vulnerabilities across three hundred and twenty-nine WordPress plugins and four themes. Match that coverage against what you've actually installed; the headline count alone won't tell you which site needs work.

I'd give the exploited infrastructure fixes protected maintenance time today, particularly where Saturday's deadline meets a holiday weekend. For the developer-account incidents, I'd check what the build and deployment path can reach beyond the repository. Hope Thursday gives you a quiet window to get the awkward changes done.
